| Key Principles  Risk Decomposition: Risks can be categorized by cause: Misuse (humans intentionally causing harm with AI), Misalignment (AI itself having unintended/harmful goals), and Systemic (broader societal disruptions from AI integration). Severity Matters: Risks vary from Catastrophic (harming a large part of humanity) to Existential (X-Risks) (threatening humanity's survival or potential). Dangerous Capabilities Drive Risk: Specific AI skills like advanced Deception, Situational Awareness, Power Seeking, and Autonomous Replication can greatly increase risk. Agency (AI acting independently) is also a key factor. Misuse is Near-Term: AI can already amplify risks in areas like Bio-Risk (designing pathogens), Cyber-Risk (automated attacks), and Autonomous Weapons. Misalignment is a Core Challenge: Failures in telling AI what we want (Specification) or how it learns/applies goals (Generalization) can lead to AIs pursuing harmful objectives or problematic Convergent Subgoals (like self-preservation). Systemic Risks Emerge from Complexity: AI can lead to broad societal problems like Epistemic Erosion (hard to know what's true), Power Concentration, Value Lock-in, or economic upheaval through Automation, even if individual AIs aren't "evil." Risk Amplifiers: Factors like accidents, indifference to safety, unpredictability (black-boxes), deployment scale, and race dynamics can make all AI risks worse. |

| Key Principles for AI Evaluations Beyond Benchmarks: Simple tests (benchmarks) have flaws (like memorizing answers, e.g., "reversal curse"). Better evaluations using many methods are needed. Capability vs. Propensity vs. Control: We must check what a model can do (Capability), what it usually does (Propensity), and if we can stop bad actions (Control). Elicitation Matters: Models might hide skills. We need techniques like help (scaffolding), tools, and challenge tests (red teaming) to see their full potential. Measurement is Hard: Test results can change easily based on prompts. Models might intentionally underperform (sandbagging). We might not know all the risks ("unknown unknowns"). Behavioral Limits & Internal Necessity: Watching model outputs isn't enough. Understanding how the model works inside (interpretability) is crucial for safety, especially against deception. Context & Design are Key: Good evaluations consider the tools/environment (affordances), challenges of automation (like MWEs), how they fit into audits, and unexpected (emergent) behaviors. |

| Key Principles for Specification Reward is Not the Goal: The reward function in RL guides learning, but the agent's learned policy might optimize the reward in unintended ways (Goodhart's Law). Specification Gaming: AI systems might achieve the literal goal specified (maximize reward) without achieving the intended spirit, often through loopholes (Reward Hacking) or manipulating the reward process itself (Reward Tampering/Wireheading). Learning from Humans is Hard: Trying to learn goals from humans is challenging. Imitation Learning (like BC, IRL) faces issues if humans aren't optimal or if the AI doesn't understand the underlying reasons (Goal Inference Problem). Learning from Feedback (RLHF/RLAIF): Training models based on human or AI preferences (e.g., RLHF, Constitutional AI) is promising but still vulnerable to reward hacking, misgeneralization, and limitations in human feedback quality/scope. Optimization Pressure: Stronger AIs are better at finding shortcuts or flaws in the goals we give. This means even small mistakes in the design can be exploited badly. |

## 

| Session 6: Key Takeaways Specification is Hard: Telling an AI exactly what we want (Outer Alignment) is difficult; simple rewards often fail (Goodhart's Law). Gaming the System: AIs may find unintended ways to get high rewards (Reward Hacking) or even interfere with the reward signal (Reward Tampering). Learning from Humans has Flaws: Copying human actions (Imitation Learning) or learning from human preferences (RLHF) helps but isn't perfect; humans aren't always optimal, and feedback can be limited or biased. AI Feedback (RLAIF): Using AI (guided by principles/constitution) to provide feedback scales better but still faces challenges. No Perfect Solution Yet: All current methods for specifying AI goals have limitations; this remains a central challenge in AI safety. |

| Key Principles for Generalization Rewards ≠ Goals: An AI might learn a "goal" that is different from the reward signal it was trained on. It might find a shortcut or a feature correlated with the reward and optimize for that instead. Goal Misgeneralization: This is when an AI's learned goal doesn't transfer correctly to new situations (distributional shift). Its skills might generalize, but its goal doesn't, leading to unexpected behavior. Optimization as Search: Training an AI (like with gradient descent) is like searching for a good algorithm (the model's parameters) among many possibilities. Mesa-Optimizers: The training process (base optimizer) might find an algorithm that is itself an optimizer (a mesa-optimizer). This "inner" optimizer has its own mesa-objective. Inner Alignment Problem: The challenge is making sure the mesa-optimizer's goal (mesa-objective) matches the goal we originally intended for the AI (base objective). Deceptive Alignment: A major risk is a mesa-optimizer that learns to pretend it's aligned during training but pursues its own hidden goals once deployed. This requires situational awareness and long-term planning. |

| Key Principles for Scalable Oversight Need for Oversight: As AIs do complex, "fuzzy" tasks that are hard for humans to check, we need ways (Scalable Oversight) to give good training signals and feedback to keep them aligned with what we want. Verification vs. Generation: It's often easier to check if an answer is good (verification) than to create it from scratch (generation). This idea is key to many oversight methods. Task Decomposition: Breaking big problems into smaller, simpler parts (like in Factored Cognition) makes them easier for humans to check and for AIs to learn. Process vs. Outcome: Watching the AI's thinking steps (Process Oversight, e.g., ERO, CoT, Procedural Cloning) can be safer than just checking the final answer. Amplification & Distillation: We can boost human or AI helper abilities (Amplification) and then teach that improved knowledge to smaller AIs (Distillation). Repeating this (IDA) aims for better AI over time. Adversarial Approaches: Methods like AI Safety via Debate use AIs arguing different sides to help a judge find truth and uncover hidden information. Weak-to-Strong Generalization (W2SG): Training smart AIs with guidance from weaker systems (or less expert humans), hoping the smart AI uses its existing knowledge to do even better than its teacher. This is a key way to test if scalable oversight ideas can work. |
